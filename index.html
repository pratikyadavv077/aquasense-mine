Active Treatment Tier
Traditional
Possible Cause

Oxidation of pyrite (FeS₂) and other sulphide minerals exposed by mining activity, generating sulphuric acid that lowers pH.

Potential Environmental Impact

Corrodes infrastructure, mobilizes toxic heavy metals (Fe, Al, Mn), harms aquatic life, and depresses pH in the receiving water body.

✉ Government / Admin routing: Central/State Pollution Control Board + District Mining Officer + Emergency Response Team
Traditional
Lime / Limestone Neutralization
Equipment / Material
  • Hydrated lime Ca(OH)₂ or crushed limestone CaCO₃
  • Slurry mixing/dosing tank
  • In-line pH probe + controller
  • Metering dosing pump
  • Reaction/retention basin
Step-by-Step Procedure
  • Continuously measure inlet pH with an in-line probe.
  • Meter lime/limestone slurry into the reaction tank proportional to acidity load.
  • Mix for 15–30 min retention time to allow neutralization to complete.
  • Let precipitated metal hydroxides (Fe, Al, Mn) settle in a clarifier.
  • Re-check pH before discharge/reuse; trim dosing if still out of range.
Advantages
  • Low capital cost, simple to operate
  • Fast-acting on strongly acidic water
  • Reagents widely available
Limitations
  • Generates metal-hydroxide sludge requiring disposal
  • Prone to overshoot without tight control
  • Ongoing reagent cost scales with acidity load
Advanced / International
Anoxic Limestone Drain (ALD) + Constructed Wetland
🌐 Passive AMD treatment used across Appalachian coalfields (USA) and at abandoned coal sites in the UK (Coal Authority mine-water schemes).
Equipment / Material
  • Buried limestone gravel drain (sealed from atmospheric O₂)
  • Constructed wetland cells (aerobic + anaerobic)
  • Settling/polishing pond
Step-by-Step Procedure
  • Route acidic water through a sealed limestone bed before it meets oxygen, preventing iron-precipitate armoring of the limestone.
  • Discharge into a constructed wetland where metals oxidize and settle as the water re-aerates.
  • Anaerobic wetland cells with organic substrate host sulphate-reducing bacteria that further raise pH and precipitate metal sulphides.
  • Water exits through a polishing pond before discharge/reuse.
Expected Benefits
  • No chemical dosing or electricity — passive, gravity-fed
  • Very low ongoing operating cost and supervision
  • Wetland habitat as an ecological co-benefit
Energy / Chemical Requirement

Near-zero — passive gravity flow; limestone charge replaced only every several years.

Advantage Over Traditional
  • No sludge handling at the point of treatment
  • Far lower long-term operating cost
  • No risk of chemical overshoot

Possible Cause

Coal fines, ore tailings ("red mud"), and excavation silt entering the water via runoff and process discharge.

Potential Environmental Impact

Blocks light penetration and smothers aquatic habitat, clogs downstream filtration/treatment equipment, and can carry adsorbed heavy metals.

✉ Government / Admin routing: Central/State Pollution Control Board + District Mining Officer + Emergency Response Team
Traditional
Sedimentation + Coagulant-Assisted Sand Filtration
Equipment / Material
  • Coagulant dosing system (alum / polyaluminium chloride)
  • Flocculation tank
  • Sedimentation clarifier
  • Multi-media sand/dual-media filter
Step-by-Step Procedure
  • Dose coagulant (alum or PAC) to destabilize suspended coal/ore particles.
  • Slow-mix in a flocculation tank to grow settleable flocs.
  • Allow flocs to settle in a sedimentation clarifier.
  • Pass clarified water through a multi-media sand filter to remove residual fines.
  • Backwash the filter periodically to restore capacity.
Advantages
  • Proven, low-tech, easy to operate and maintain
  • Handles moderate turbidity loads well
  • Low equipment cost
Limitations
  • Struggles with extreme "red mud"-type loads (>400 NTU)
  • Coagulant sludge requires disposal
  • Backwash water needs further handling
Advanced / International
Electrocoagulation (Fe/Al Electrodes) + Ceramic Micro/Ultrafiltration
🌐 Electrocoagulation is used at mine tailings-water facilities in Australia and Canada; ceramic UF is established in industrial water-reuse plants in Japan and the EU.
Equipment / Material
  • Electrocoagulation cell (sacrificial Fe/Al electrode plates, DC supply)
  • Flotation/settling stage for coagulated floc
  • Ceramic micro/ultrafiltration membrane skid
Step-by-Step Procedure
  • Pass water through an electrocoagulation cell; applied current dissolves Fe/Al electrodes in situ, generating coagulant without bulk chemical storage.
  • Fine electrolysis gas bubbles float coagulated particles for easy removal (electroflotation).
  • Polish the effluent through ceramic micro/ultrafiltration to reach sub-0.1 NTU permeate.
  • Periodically air-scour/backwash the membranes to maintain flux.
Expected Benefits
  • Achieves permeate turbidity below 0.1 NTU
  • No bulk chemical storage/handling on site
  • Compact footprint versus conventional clarifiers
Energy / Chemical Requirement

Electrical only, typically 0.5–2 kWh/m³ depending on particle load; electrodes are consumed and replaced periodically.

Advantage Over Traditional
  • Far higher, more consistent removal on extreme loads
  • No liquid coagulant supply chain
  • Smaller physical footprint

Possible Cause

Leaching of sulphates, chlorides and dissolved metals from exposed ore and waste rock, plus residual process chemicals.

Potential Environmental Impact

Degrades water for irrigation/drinking use, contributes to salinization of the receiving water body, and accelerates equipment corrosion.

✉ Government / Admin routing: State Pollution Control Board + Mine Safety Officer
Traditional
Dilution / Conventional Coagulation-Settling
Equipment / Material
  • Dilution/blending tank (where a lower-TDS source exists)
  • Coagulant dosing (co-precipitates some dissolved metals)
  • Settling basin
Step-by-Step Procedure
  • Blend high-TDS water with a lower-TDS source where available.
  • Dose coagulant to co-precipitate a portion of dissolved metals with suspended solids.
  • Settle and decant.
  • Re-check TDS/EC — true dissolved salts (sulphates, chlorides) remain largely unaffected.
Advantages
  • Very low cost where a dilution source exists
  • Simple to add to existing sedimentation infrastructure
Limitations
  • Does not remove dissolved salts, only dilutes them
  • Ineffective alone for sulphate-heavy AMD or process-salt water
  • Requires a secondary water source, not always available
Advanced / International
Multi-Stage Reverse Osmosis (UF Pre-treatment + RO)
🌐 Used for coal-seam-gas produced-water treatment in Australia and AMD reclamation projects in South Africa.
Equipment / Material
  • Ultrafiltration pre-treatment skid
  • High-pressure RO pump
  • Multi-stage RO membrane array
  • Concentrate/brine management line
Step-by-Step Procedure
  • Pre-treat with ultrafiltration to protect RO membranes from fouling.
  • Pressurize and pass water through RO membranes; permeate exits as low-TDS clean water.
  • Route the concentrated reject/brine stream to a separate management step.
  • Monitor membrane differential pressure and periodically clean-in-place (CIP) to control fouling/scaling.
Expected Benefits
  • Removes 90%+ of dissolved salts including sulphates and most dissolved metals
  • Produces a reusable-quality permeate stream
  • Commercially mature, well-proven technology
Energy / Chemical Requirement

Energy-intensive, typically 1–3 kWh/m³ depending on feed TDS and pressure; needs periodic antiscalant and CIP chemicals.

Advantage Over Traditional
  • Actually removes dissolved salts rather than diluting them
  • Defined permeate quality suitable for reuse
  • Scales to continuous, unattended operation

✉ Government / Admin routing: Mine Administration + Environmental Compliance Officer
Traditional
Passive Heat Dissipation / Cooling Ponds
Equipment / Material
  • Cooling pond or cascade aerator
  • Temperature sensor with boundary alarm
Step-by-Step Procedure
  • Route water through a cooling pond or cascade structure to lose heat to ambient air.
  • Continuously monitor discharge temperature against the permitted boundary.
  • Trigger a boundary alarm if temperature remains outside range after passive cooling.
Advantages
  • No equipment cost beyond a pond/structure
  • No energy input required
Limitations
  • Slow and weather-dependent, large land footprint
  • No value recovered from the thermal energy — it is simply dissipated
Advanced / International
Mine-Water Heat Recovery via Heat Exchanger
🌐 Operating examples include the Heerlen minewater geothermal network (Netherlands) and UK Coal Authority mine-water heat schemes at Gateshead and Seaham.
Equipment / Material
  • Plate or shell-and-tube heat exchanger
  • Secondary heating/cooling distribution loop
  • Circulation pumps
Step-by-Step Procedure
  • Pass warm mine water through one side of a heat exchanger.
  • Transfer heat to a clean secondary loop feeding a district heating network, greenhouse, or building HVAC.
  • Return the now-cooled mine water to the treatment/discharge line.
  • Monitor exchanger performance and clean periodically to prevent scaling/fouling losses.
Expected Benefits
  • Recovers usable thermal energy instead of wasting it
  • Corrects the discharge-temperature excursion as a side effect
  • Can offset heating/cooling costs for nearby facilities
Energy / Chemical Requirement

Circulation-pump power only, no chemicals; net energy-positive when nearby heat demand exists.

Advantage Over Traditional
  • Turns a compliance problem into an energy asset
  • Faster, more controllable correction than passive ponds
